= Busbar: Joule Heating
:description: Build the busbar model of the COMSOL introduction in Elmer Studio: electric currents heat a copper busbar bolted with titanium bolts.
:page-pagination:

A copper busbar carries a direct current between three titanium bolts.
The current heats the bar (Joule heating) and the bar loses that heat to the surrounding air by convection.
You build the geometry from parameters, solve the coupled electric and thermal problem, then sweep the busbar width and plot how the average temperature falls as the bar gets wider.

.What you will learn
* Set up a multiphysics model with the Model Wizard (Joule Heating).
* Sketch on a work plane, round corners, and extrude; place work planes on faces of the part you built.
* Assign two materials and apply boundary conditions by clicking faces.
* Use a user-controlled mesh driven by a parameter.
* Run a parametric sweep and evaluate a coupling operator (`+aveop1(T)+`) over all sweep values.

.Model at a glance
[cols="1,2"]
|===
|Space dimension |3D
|Physics |Electric Currents (ec) + Heat Transfer in Solids (ht), coupled by Electromagnetic Heating
|Study |Stationary, with a Parametric Sweep over the width `+wbb+` (6 values)
|Mesh |Quadratic tetrahedra; about 37 000 nodes at `+wbb+` = 5 cm, growing to about 77 000 at 10 cm
|Solve time |6 to 8 minutes for the whole sweep on a desktop PC (two recorded runs: 350 s and 447 s)
|===

TIP: If you know COMSOL, this is the busbar from _Introduction to COMSOL Multiphysics_. The workflow is the same; the differences are called out in notes along the way. Elmer Studio is an independent project and is not affiliated with COMSOL.

== Start a new model

. Click menu:File[New] (kbd:[Ctrl+N]) and choose *Model Wizard*.
. On *Select Space Dimension*, choose *3D*.
. On *Select Physics*, expand *Heat Transfer › Electromagnetic Heating*, select *Joule Heating* and click *Add*.
The list on the right now shows Electric Currents (ec) and Heat Transfer in Solids (ht).
. Click *Study*, select *General Studies › Stationary* and click *Done*.

The Model Builder now contains *Component 1* with *Geometry 1*, *Materials*, *Electric Currents (ec)*, *Heat Transfer in Solids (ht)*, *Multiphysics* (with the Electromagnetic Heating coupling), *Mesh 1* and *Study 1*.
Elmer Studio has already added the default features: *Current Conservation 1*, *Electric Insulation 1*, *Solid 1*, *Thermal Insulation 1* and the *Initial Values* nodes.

== Parameters

. On the *Home* ribbon, click menu:Home[Parameters]. This selects *Parameters 1* under *Global Definitions*.
. In the Settings window, type the following rows into the table. Each new row appears when you start typing in the empty last row; the _Value_ column fills in by itself.
+
[cols="1,2,3",options="header"]
|===
|Name |Expression |Description
|L |`+9[cm]+` |Length of lower part of the busbar
|rad_1 |`+6[mm]+` |Radius of the titanium bolts
|tbb |`+5[mm]+` |Thickness of the busbar
|wbb |`+5[cm]+` |Width of the busbar
|mh |`+3[mm]+` |Maximum element size
|htc |`+5[W/(m^2*K)]+` |Heat transfer coefficient
|Vtot |`+20[mV]+` |Applied electric potential
|===

TIP: The *Load from File* and *Save to File* buttons under the table read and write the list as a text file with one parameter per line (name, expression, description, separated by spaces).

== Geometry

The busbar is an L-shaped profile drawn in the xz-plane and extruded by the width `+wbb+`.
The bolts are circles drawn on faces of that extruded bar and extruded through it.
The geometry stays in the default length unit, metres; all sizes come from the parameters.
See xref:user-guide:geometry.adoc[Geometry] for how work planes, building and entity numbers work.

=== Work Plane 1: the L profile

. Click *Geometry 1*, then click menu:Geometry[Work Plane].
. In the Settings of *Work Plane 1*, keep _Plane type_ `+Quick+` and set _Plane_ to `+xz-plane+`.
. Right-click *Work Plane 1* and choose *Primitives › Rectangle*. In *Rectangle 1* set _Width_ `+L+2*tbb+` and _Height_ `+0.1+`. Leave the position at `+0+`, `+0+`.
. Right-click *Work Plane 1* again and choose *Primitives › Rectangle*. In *Rectangle 2* set _Width_ `+L+tbb+`, _Height_ `+0.1-tbb+`, _x_ `+0+` and _y_ `+tbb+`.
. Right-click *Work Plane 1* and choose *Booleans and Partitions › Difference*. In *Difference 1* check `+r1+` under _Objects to add_ and `+r2+` under _Objects to subtract_.
. Press kbd:[F7] (*Build Selected*) to build the work plane up to this node.

Next, round the inner corner of the L with radius `+tbb+` and the outer corner of the bend with radius `+2*tbb+`.
A plane fillet refers to the corners by point number. Points are numbered by position, x first, then y: in the L profile, point 3 is the inner corner at (`+95[mm]+`, `+5[mm]+`).

. Right-click *Work Plane 1* and choose *Conversions › Fillet* (or, with a node of the work plane selected, click menu:Geometry[Fillet]). *Fillet 1* is added to the work plane. The Graphics window shows the plane sketch built so far, with its points numbered.
. Click point *3*, the inner corner, in the Graphics window (or type `+3+` into _Vertices to fillet (point numbers, if not picked)_), and set _Radius_ to `+tbb+`.
+
.The plane sketch at Fillet 1: the L profile with its six corner points numbered by position
image::ROOT:screenshots/tutorial_busbar_1_workplane_fillet_points.png["Settings of Fillet 1 with point 3 and radius tbb; the Graphics window shows the L profile with points 1 to 6",role=screenshot]
. Add a second fillet the same way. In *Fillet 2*, pick point *6* and set _Radius_ to `+2*tbb+`. Point 6 is the outer corner of the bend at (`+100[mm]+`, `+0+`). After Fillet 1, the rounded inner corner has two end points instead of one, so the corners further right move up by one number. The sketch at Fillet 2 shows the new numbers.

TIP: In 2D models and inside work planes, *Fillet* and *Chamfer* act on the sketch's corner points. In a 3D geometry sequence, they act on the edges of a solid.

=== Extrude 1: the bar

. Click *Fillet 2*, then click menu:Geometry[Extrude]. *Extrude 1* is added after the work plane with `+wp1+` checked under _Input objects_.
. Set _Distance from work plane_ to `+wbb+`.
. Press kbd:[F7]. The bar is 100 mm high, 100 mm long and `+wbb+` wide.

=== Work Plane 2: the upper bolt

. With *Extrude 1* selected, click menu:Geometry[Work Plane].
. In *Work Plane 2* set _Plane type_ to `+Face parallel+`. The Graphics window shows the geometry built so far with its boundary numbers.
. Type `+8+` into _Planar face (boundary number)_. Boundary 8 is the inner face of the vertical leg, at x = 95 mm; it turns blue in the Graphics window. Instead of typing, you can click that face: a picked face appears in the _Boundary Selection_ list and takes precedence over the typed number.
+
.Picking the face for the face-parallel work plane: boundary 8 is highlighted
image::ROOT:screenshots/tutorial_busbar_2_face_parallel_pick.png["Settings of Work Plane 2 with Plane type Face parallel and planar face 8; the Graphics window shows the extruded L with boundary numbers and face 8 highlighted",role=screenshot]
. Right-click *Work Plane 2* and choose *Primitives › Circle*. Set _Radius_ to `+rad_1+`. Keep _x_ and _y_ at `+0+`: the work plane's origin is the centre of face 8.
. Click *Circle 1*, then click menu:Geometry[Extrude]. *Extrude 2* takes `+wp2+` as input. Set _Distance from work plane_ to `+-2*tbb+`.
+
The face normal points out of the bar, so a negative distance extrudes the circle into the vertical leg and `+tbb+` out of its far side.

=== Work Plane 3: the two lower bolts

. With *Extrude 2* selected, click menu:Geometry[Work Plane].
. In *Work Plane 3* set _Plane type_ to `+Face parallel+` and type `+4+` into _Planar face (boundary number)_ (or click the face). Boundary 4 is the top face of the horizontal leg.
. Right-click *Work Plane 3* and choose *Primitives › Circle*. Set _Radius_ `+rad_1+`, _x_ `+-L/2+1.5[cm]+` and _y_ `+-wbb/4+`.
. Right-click *Work Plane 3* and choose *Transforms › Copy*. In *Copy 1* check the circle (`+c2+`) under _Input objects_ and set the displacement _y_ to `+wbb/2+`.
. Click *Copy 1*, then click menu:Geometry[Extrude]. *Extrude 3* takes `+wp3+` as input. Set _Distance from work plane_ to `+-2*tbb+`.
. Click *Geometry 1* and press kbd:[F8] (*Build All Objects*).

The geometry has 7 domains: domain 1 is the copper bar and domains 2–7 are the bolts.
Each bolt is split in two where it passes the surface of the bar.
*Form Union* at the end of the sequence joins everything into one mesh-compatible assembly.

TIP: Save now with kbd:[Ctrl+S]. Models are stored as `+.esm+` files.

== Materials

. Click menu:Home[Add Material]. The *Add Material* window opens on the right.
. Select *Built-in › Copper* and click *Add to Component*. The first material you add covers all domains.
. Select *Metals › Titanium beta-21S* and click *Add to Component*. This material starts with an empty selection.
. In the Settings of *Titanium beta-21S*, click the six bolt domains 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7 in the Graphics window. They turn blue and appear in the _Domain Selection_ list.
*Transparency* in the Graphics toolbar helps to see the bolt parts inside the bar.

The Material Contents table marks every property the physics needs with a check mark: density, thermal conductivity, heat capacity and electrical conductivity.
*Copper* still lists all domains; as in COMSOL, where two materials share a domain, the one lower in the Model Builder is used.

== Physics

Current enters through the end face of the upper bolt and leaves through the bottom faces of the two lower bolts.
Every other outside surface loses heat by convection.

=== Electric Currents

. Click *Electric Currents (ec)* in the Model Builder.
. Click menu:Physics[Boundaries > Electric Potential].
. In *Electric Potential 1*, click boundary 43, the end face of the upper bolt sticking out of the vertical leg. Set _Electric potential_ to `+Vtot+`.
. Click *Electric Currents (ec)* again and click menu:Physics[Boundaries > Ground].
. In *Ground 1*, click boundaries 7 and 14, the bottom faces of the two lower bolts.

TIP: To rotate the view and reach the bottom faces, drag with the left mouse button; kbd:[F5] zooms back to the whole model. Clicking a selected entity again removes it from the selection.

All remaining boundaries keep the default *Electric Insulation 1*.
The settings are listed in xref:reference:physics-ec.adoc[].

=== Heat Transfer in Solids

. Click *Heat Transfer in Solids (ht)* and click menu:Physics[Boundaries > Heat Flux].
. In *Heat Flux 1*, set _Flux type_ to `+Convective heat flux+`, _Heat transfer coefficient_ to `+htc+` and _External temperature_ to `+293.15[K]+` (the default).
. Select the outside surfaces except the three bolt contact faces:
.. Click *Select All* in the Graphics window toolbar. All 43 boundaries are added to the list.
.. In the _Boundary Selection_ list, hold kbd:[Ctrl] and click `+7+`, `+8+`, `+9+`, `+12+`, `+14+`, `+15+`, `+16+`, `+19+`, `+21+`, `+23+`, `+25+`, `+27+`, `+33+`, `+34+`, `+35+`, `+36+`, `+38+` and `+43+`.
.. Click the *Remove* button (the minus sign) next to the list. 25 boundaries remain.

NOTE: In COMSOL, a boundary condition with all boundaries selected acts only on the exterior ones. In Elmer Studio, only the default features (such as Thermal Insulation) are restricted to exterior boundaries; a feature you add acts on every boundary in its list. The 15 boundaries removed above besides 7, 14 and 43 are the interfaces between the bolts and the bar, which must not lose heat.

The Joule heat source needs no setup: the *Electromagnetic Heating* coupling under *Multiphysics* feeds the resistive losses of Electric Currents into Heat Transfer on all domains.
See xref:reference:physics-ht.adoc[] and xref:user-guide:physics.adoc[].

== Mesh

The mesh is controlled by the parameter `+mh+`.

. On the *Mesh* ribbon, click menu:Mesh[Free Tetrahedral]. *Mesh 1* switches to a user-controlled sequence and gets two nodes, *Size 1* and *Free Tetrahedral 1*.
. Click *Size 1*. Set _Element size_ to `+Custom+`, then set:
** _Maximum element size_: `+mh+`
** _Minimum element size_: `+mh-mh/3+`
** _Curvature factor_: `+0.2+`
. Click menu:Mesh[Build Mesh]. The Messages window reports the number of elements and nodes.

Elements are quadratic, because both physics interfaces use quadratic discretization by default.
See xref:user-guide:mesh.adoc[] for the other size settings.

== Study

First solve one width, then add the sweep.

. Click *Study 1* and click menu:Home[Compute] (kbd:[Ctrl+=]). The Progress window and a Convergence Plot tab show the solver at work; three default plot groups appear under *Results* when it finishes.
. To sweep the width, click menu:Study[Parametric Sweep]. *Parametric Sweep 1* is added at the top of *Study 1*.
. Set _Parameter name_ to `+wbb+`, _Parameter value list_ to `+range(5,1,10)+` and _Parameter unit_ to `+cm+`.
That is six widths from 5 cm to 10 cm in steps of 1 cm.

The geometry and mesh are rebuilt for every width. Before computing, add the coupling operator you will evaluate afterwards.

. Right-click *Definitions* under *Component 1* and choose *Average*.
. In *Average 1*, set _Operator name_ to `+aveop1+`. Leave the _Domain Selection_ at all domains.
. Click menu:Home[Compute]. The Messages window lists each sweep step, for example `+Parametric sweep: wbb = 0.1 (6/6)+`.

TIP: The operator defined under Definitions works in any result expression: `+aveop1(T)+` is the volume average of the temperature over the selected domains, like COMSOL's Average component coupling.

See xref:user-guide:studies.adoc[] and xref:user-guide:definitions.adoc[].

== Results

After the sweep, the plot groups show the last parameter value (`+wbb+` = 10 cm) unless you change _Solution selection_ in the plot group.

=== Temperature and potential

. Click *Temperature (ht)* to see the surface temperature. The top bolt, where the current enters, is the hottest part.
. Click *Electric Potential (ec)*. The Multislice plot shows the potential falling from 20 mV at the upper bolt to 0 at the two grounded bolts.

=== Average temperature versus width

. Click menu:Results[Global Evaluation]. In *Global Evaluation 1*:
** _Dataset_: `+Study 1/Solution 1+`
** _Time/parameter selection_: `+All+` (the default)
** In the _Expressions_ table, type `+aveop1(T)+` in the _Expression_ column and `+Average temperature+` in _Description_.
. Click *Evaluate* at the top of the Settings window. The Table window lists one row per width.
. Click menu:Results[Global]. A *1D Plot Group* with a *Global 1* plot is added.
. In *Global 1*, set _Expression (global, e.g. aveop1(T), or a field to reduce)_ to `+aveop1(T)+` and _Reduction_ to `+Global (scalar expression)+`.
. Click *Plot*. The x-axis is the sweep parameter, `+wbb (cm)+`.

== Check the results

These are the checks in `+tests/test_tutorial_busbar.py+`, which builds this model step by step and runs the sweep; they follow what the COMSOL tutorial text states.

* The geometry has *7 domains*; domains 2–7 are the bolts.
* At `+wbb+` = 5 cm the maximum temperature is *above 300 K*, well above the ambient 293.15 K (about 330.5 K).
* The temperature difference across the whole device is *less than 10 K* (8.0 K at 5 cm, 8.5 K at 10 cm).
* The *average temperature decreases* with every step of the width sweep: about 322.8 K at 5 cm, 318.5 K at 6 cm, 315.3 K at 7 cm, 312.8 K at 8 cm, 310.9 K at 9 cm and 309.2 K at 10 cm.

The values in brackets come from a verification run of the test model; your last digit may differ because the mesh is generated afresh.

The project README lists two further physical checks:

* The top bolt is the hottest part: the maximum temperature sits on the end face of the upper bolt, where the current enters.
* The Joule power generated in the bar equals the heat lost by convection to within 0.4 % (about 3.2 W at 5 cm; the verification run gave 0.3 % at every width).

To check the power balance yourself, compare a Volume Integration of `+ec.Qrh+` (the Joule loss density) with a Surface Integration of `+htc*(T-293.15[K])+` over the 25 convective boundaries.

* menu:File[New] lists the *Busbar* example in the Application Libraries. It is a simpler busbar built from blocks, with bolt holes but no bolts, that solves in a few seconds.
